## Explanation

Suppose:

nums = [1, 2]

Binary:

1 = 01
2 = 10

There are 2^2 = 4 subsets:

[]      → XOR = 0
[1]     → XOR = 1
[2]     → XOR = 2
[1,2]   → XOR = 1 ^ 2 = 3

Sum:

0 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 6

Now look at the bits one bit at a time.

Look at the rightmost bit

The numbers are:

01
10

For the rightmost bit:

1 has this bit
2 does not

Now look at the 4 subset XORs:

0 → 00   → right bit = 0
1 → 01   → right bit = 1
2 → 10   → right bit = 0
3 → 11   → right bit = 1

See it?

0, 1, 0, 1

The bit is 1 in 2 out of 4 subsets.

So this bit contributes:

2 subsets × value of bit(1)
= 2 × 1
= 2
Now look at the second bit

The second bit is worth 2.

Again, across all subsets:

0 → 00   → 0
1 → 01   → 0
2 → 10   → 1
3 → 11   → 1

Again it is 1 in exactly 2 out of 4 subsets.

So this bit contributes:

2 subsets × 2
= 4

Total:

2 + 4 = 6

Which is the answer.

So where does 2^(n-1) come from?

There are:

2^n

subsets.

For each bit that exists in at least one number, that bit will be 1 in exactly half of all subsets.

Half of:

2^n

is:

2^(n-1)

That's all that formula means.

Why do we use OR?

Suppose:

nums = [1, 2]

OR them:

01
10
--
11

So:

1 | 2 = 3

This tells us:

Which bit positions exist in at least one number?

Here both bits exist:

11

Both contribute.

Then:

OR = 3

and each of those bits appears in:

2^(2-1) = 2

subsets.

So:

3 × 2 = 6
Another example: [1, 3]
1 = 01
3 = 11

OR:

01
11
--
11

So:

OR = 3

There are 2 elements, so each bit appears in 2 subsets.

Therefore:

3 × 2 = 6
### The easiest way to remember it

Don't memorize:

OR * 2^(n-1)
