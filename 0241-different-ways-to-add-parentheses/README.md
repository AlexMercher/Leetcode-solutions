## Explanation

DP becomes useful because the same subexpression can be evaluated in many different ways.

The key idea

Suppose:

2 * 3 - 4 * 5

Pick an operator as the final operator:

2 * | 3 - 4 * 5

Then every possible result is:

every result from left × every result from right

Or pick:

2 * 3 - | 4 * 5

and combine the left and right results using subtraction.

So define:

solve(l, r)

as:

All possible results obtainable from the expression between l and r.

For every operator between l and r:

leftResults  = solve(l, op-1)
rightResults = solve(op+1, r)

Then take their Cartesian product:

for a in leftResults
    for b in rightResults
        combine(a, b, operator)
Why this is DP-like

Consider:

2 * 3 - 4 * 5

The subexpression:

3 - 4

can appear in multiple different parenthesizations.

So instead of recalculating:

solve(3,4)

again and again, memoize it.

That gives:

expression
   ↓
choose an operator as the final split
   ↓
left subproblem + right subproblem
   ↓
recursively solve both
   ↓
combine EVERY left result with EVERY right result
This is different from ordinary backtracking

Backtracking usually looks like:

choose
  ↓
explore
  ↓
undo

Here there isn't really a mutable choice being undone. We are partitioning an expression around every possible operator and combining independently generated result sets.

So I would classify it as:

Divide & Conquer
      +
Memoization
      +
Interval / Partition DP

How to recognize it

When you see:

"Find all possible results for all possible ways of parenthesizing/grouping an expression."

think immediately:

Choose a possible final operator
        ↓
Split expression into LEFT and RIGHT
        ↓
Solve both independently
        ↓
Combine every left result with every right result

This is one of the strongest signals for partition/interval-style DP with recursion.
